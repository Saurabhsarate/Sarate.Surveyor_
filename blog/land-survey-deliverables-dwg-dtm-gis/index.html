Land Survey Deliverables Explained: DWG, DTM, GIS Data and Elevation Information

Understanding the surveying outputs that support engineering design, construction planning and infrastructure development.

A successful engineering or infrastructure project depends on reliable information about the site and its surrounding conditions.

Land surveying plays an important role in collecting this information. However, collecting field measurements is only one part of the surveying process. The information must also be organised, processed and presented in suitable formats so that engineers, architects, planners and project teams can use it effectively.

This is where land survey deliverables become important.

Depending on the project requirements, a surveying assignment may include CAD drawings, Digital Terrain Models (DTM), GIS data, shapefiles, contour maps, elevation information and technical reports.

Understanding these deliverables helps project stakeholders define their requirements before starting a surveying assignment.

1. What Are Land Survey Deliverables?

Land survey deliverables are the final outputs provided after completing the required field data collection, processing and mapping activities.

They communicate the surveyed site's physical characteristics, measurements, coordinates, elevations and other relevant spatial information.

The exact deliverables depend on several factors:

  • Purpose of the survey: The underlying engineering, architectural or administrative goal.
  • Project location and site conditions: Accessibility, physical topography, and vegetation cover.
  • Type and extent of surveying required: Whether boundary demarcation, topographical profiling, or utility corridor routing.
  • Engineering and planning requirements: Design tolerances, BIM specifications, and local development authority bylaws.
  • Required file formats: Compatibility with software platforms such as AutoCAD, Civil 3D, ArcGIS, or QGIS.
  • Project scope and specifications: Scale, grid intervals, and detail density.

For example, a topographic survey for a construction site may require contour drawings and terrain information, whereas a pipeline alignment survey may require longitudinal sections, cross-sections and alignment information.

💡 Engineering Consideration

Defining the required deliverables before beginning the survey is an important part of project planning. Miscommunication regarding required coordinate reference systems or CAD layer formatting can lead to costly delays during design integration.

2. CAD Drawings (DWG): A Foundation for Engineering Design

CAD drawings are among the commonly requested outputs of land surveying assignments.

DWG files can present surveyed information in a structured drawing format that engineering professionals can review and use within compatible CAD software.

Depending on the survey scope, CAD drawings may contain:

  • Surveyed site boundaries and demarcation corners.
  • Existing buildings, permanent compound walls, and structures.
  • Roads, curb lines, and access routes.
  • Ground features, vegetation, trees, and physical details.
  • Contour lines indicating topographic gradient.
  • Spot levels and elevation information across the grid.
  • Relevant project-specific survey details and utility infrastructure.

Why are CAD drawings important?

They provide engineers and planners with a drawing-based representation of surveyed site conditions.

These drawings can support design development, site planning, technical review and coordination between project stakeholders. For projects in Maharashtra and across India, CAD drawings provide the geometric foundation required for municipal submissions, structural column grid layouts, and earthwork estimations.

The content and level of detail should always match the agreed survey scope.

3. Digital Terrain Models (DTM): Understanding Ground Conditions

A Digital Terrain Model (DTM) represents the terrain surface digitally.

It provides information about the ground's shape and elevation characteristics and can support engineering and infrastructure planning.

Depending on the survey requirements and processing methodology, a DTM may help identify:

  • Changes in ground elevation across complex terrain.
  • Terrain variations, undulations, and natural depressions.
  • Slopes and surface gradients essential for stability analysis.
  • Natural ground characteristics and flow lines.
  • Areas requiring further engineering assessment, grading, or retaining structures.

Where can DTM information be useful?

DTM information can support:

  • Site development planning: Evaluating cut and fill earthwork balances to minimize haulage costs.
  • Road and infrastructure design: Establishing longitudinal gradients and transverse cross-sections.
  • Terrain analysis: Identifying steep slopes, catchment ridges, and natural drainage paths.
  • Earthwork-related assessments: Precise volumetric calculations for billing and contractor coordination.
  • Drainage and grading studies: Preventing waterlogging and designing stormwater channels.

The quality and suitability of the model depend on the survey coverage, measurement methodology, data processing and project specifications.

4. GIS Data and Shapefiles: Working with Geospatial Information

Geographic Information System (GIS) data allows spatial information to be organised, visualised and analysed geographically.

Shapefiles (.shp) and geospatial databases are commonly used GIS data formats across infrastructure and urban planning authorities.

Depending on the project requirements, GIS datasets may represent:

  • Surveyed boundaries and cadastral revenue land parcels (Gat/Survey numbers).
  • Road networks, transportation corridors, and infrastructure features.
  • Pipeline alignments for oil, gas, and city water networks.
  • Land-use features, environmental buffer zones, and water bodies.
  • Geographic reference information tied to universal coordinate systems (UTM/WGS84).
  • Other spatially mapped features with structured attributes.

Why is GIS information useful?

GIS data can support spatial analysis, geographic visualisation and integration with other relevant datasets.

For infrastructure projects covering larger geographical areas—such as cross-country pipelines or highway expansions—structured geospatial information can help project teams understand the relationship between different mapped features, property boundaries, and terrain restrictions.

The coordinate reference system and data structure should be agreed upon before delivery.

5. Elevation Information and Contour Maps

Elevation information describes the vertical position of surveyed ground points relative to an agreed reference datum, such as Mean Sea Level (MSL) or an established project benchmark.

Contour maps represent points of equal elevation through contour lines, offering an intuitive visual representation of the site's slope and relief.

Typical elevation deliverables include:

  • Spot levels: Specific elevation points recorded on a regular grid or at critical breaks in slope.
  • Contour lines: Major and minor contour intervals (e.g., 0.5m, 1.0m, or 5.0m depending on terrain).
  • Longitudinal profiles (L-sections): Elevation profiles along the centerline of a proposed route.
  • Cross-sectional profiles (C-sections): Transverse elevation slices perpendicular to the centerline.
  • Ground elevation variations: Benchmarks referenced to national geodetic datums.
  • Relevant reference levels: Invert levels of stormwater drains, manholes, and adjoining road crowns.

Applications of Elevation Data

Key applications include:

  • Construction planning: Setting finished floor levels (FFL) relative to surrounding roads.
  • Road geometric design support: Achieving safe stopping sight distances and manageable gradients.
  • Pipeline alignment assessment: Ensuring gravity flow or designing pumping lift requirements for water pipeline infrastructure and gas pipeline surveys.
  • Site grading studies: Preventing storm runoff pooling on industrial or residential plots.
  • Terrain evaluation: Verifying rock outcroppings or deep hollows before foundation design.

For projects involving significant elevation changes, properly specified elevation information is an important input for engineering assessment.

6. Survey Reports and Supporting Documentation

In addition to drawings and digital datasets, certain projects require supporting technical documentation.

Depending on the agreed scope, a comprehensive survey report may include:

  • Survey methodology: Field procedures, control network establishment, and traverse loops.
  • Project and site information: Client details, location description, survey dates, and environmental conditions.
  • Coordinate reference details: Datum used (WGS84, Everest 1830), projection (UTM Zone 43N), and permanent control point descriptions.
  • Equipment and measurement information: Make, model, and calibration certificates of Total Stations, DGPS/RTK receivers, or digital levels.
  • Survey observations: Raw observation files, closure errors, and precision checks.
  • Drawing references: Master drawing sheet register and revision histories.
  • Relevant technical notes: Disclaimers, observed physical limitations, and boundary monument descriptions.

Documentation helps stakeholders understand what information was collected, how it was presented and what the supplied outputs represent.

The required reporting format should be discussed before the project begins.

7. How to Select the Right Survey Deliverables for Your Project

Not every project requires every available survey output. The appropriate deliverables depend on the intended application, project scale, and engineering discipline.

Residential and Commercial Development

Potential requirements include topographic drawings, contours, spot levels, existing compound wall checks, and terrain information. Before breaking ground on a residential plot, property owners should also review our guide on 7 essential land survey checks before construction to safeguard boundary setbacks and foundation levels.

Highway and Road Projects

Potential requirements include longitudinal sections, transverse sections, geometric measurements, pavement edge spot levels, and elevation profiles adhering to Indian Roads Congress (IRC) standards.

Gas and Oil Pipeline Projects

Potential requirements include route alignment information, L-sections, cross-sections, utility crossing clash detection, and Right of Way (ROU) corridor demarcation. Learn more about our specialized capabilities in gas pipeline surveying.

Water Pipeline Infrastructure

Potential requirements include pipeline route alignment information, hydraulic gradient elevation mapping, invert level recording, and supporting CAD drawings for municipal networks and irrigation channels.

Industrial and Infrastructure Development

Potential requirements include comprehensive site mapping, 3D terrain models, existing feature inventories, and project-specific spatial datasets for master planning and environmental clearances.

These are indicative applications. The final deliverables should always be determined by the project's technical requirements and agreed scope.

8. Important Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Surveying Company

Before appointing a surveying service provider, project stakeholders should clarify the following:

  1. What type of survey is required? (e.g., boundary demarcation, topographic mapping, route alignment, or level survey).
  2. What area or project length needs to be surveyed? (hectares, square meters, or linear kilometers).
  3. Which equipment and methodology are appropriate? (DGPS/RTK, Total Station, UAV drone, or digital levelling).
  4. What coordinate reference system will be used? (Local grid, UTM/WGS84, or national geodetic datum).
  5. Will the final drawings be provided in DWG format? (compatible with AutoCAD or Civil 3D versions).
  6. Is a DTM or GIS dataset required? (confirm whether 3D surface files or shapefiles are needed).
  7. Will elevation information and technical documentation be included? (spot level density and contour intervals).
  8. What is the expected project timeline? (field data collection schedule and post-processing turnaround).
  9. Are the deliverables aligned with the engineering team's requirements? (confirm layer naming and pen-table standards with your design architect or structural engineer).

Discussing these points in advance helps reduce misunderstandings and ensures that the survey scope is aligned with the intended application.

Conclusion

A land surveying assignment should be planned around the information that the project team actually needs.

CAD drawings, Digital Terrain Models, GIS datasets, contour maps and elevation information serve different purposes. Selecting the appropriate outputs helps make surveyed information more useful for engineering assessment, planning and project coordination.

Before beginning your next surveying assignment, define the survey scope and required deliverables clearly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main deliverables of a land survey? +

Depending on the project, deliverables may include CAD drawings, contour maps, DTM, GIS datasets, elevation information and technical reports.

What is the difference between CAD drawings and DTM? +

CAD drawings present surveyed information in a drawing format, whereas a DTM digitally represents the terrain surface.

What is a shapefile in surveying? +

A shapefile is a GIS data format used to store and represent geographic features and their associated attributes.

Are GIS data and CAD drawings the same? +

No. CAD drawings are primarily used for technical drafting and design workflows, while GIS datasets support geographic information management and spatial analysis.

What information is required to request a land survey quotation? +

Typically, you should provide the project location, survey type, approximate area or length, project purpose and required deliverables.
